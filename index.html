Tonal Gridworld

A demo that makes Q-learning, a basic reinforcement-learning algorithm, visible and audible. To hear a trained agent, press +2,000 episodes twice, then Play.

An agent teaches itself to play music. Each square of the 8×8 grid is a note, from C2 at the bottom left to D♯7 at the top right: one semitone higher for each square to the right, eight for each row up. The agent plays 8-step sequences and learns by Q-learning, from trial, error and points alone. Nobody tells it which notes belong to the scale.

Moves Like a chess king: one square in any of eight directions, but never straight back to the square it just left.

Points Notes in the chosen scale are safe; notes outside it cost points. And because we want each sequence to sound like it belongs to the scale, some steps of a sequence ask for one particular note. So the same square can be worth different points at different moments. How it works, below, explains the details.

How it works

01 Scale and sequence

A scale is the set of notes a piece of music uses. The three here each keep seven of the twelve notes in an octave, all built on C. A few of them carry the key:

  • Tonic, C: home. A melody feels finished when it lands there.
  • Dominant, G: the 5th degree, the strongest note after the tonic.
  • Third: E in major and Phrygian dominant, E♭ in harmonic minor. It decides whether the music sounds major or minor.
  • Leading tone, B: a semitone below C, it pulls up into it. Harmonic minor raises its 7th to B for this pull; without it, C minor shares six notes with E♭ major and can sound like it.

A scale says which notes to use but not when. A sequence does: a row of steps, one note each, played in a loop as in a step sequencer. Here every 8-step sequence has the same shape, marked by the bars in the figure:

  • Step 1: the third, so you hear major or minor at once.
  • Step 4: the dominant, G.
  • Step 7, in two scales: a note a semitone from C that resolves into it. B rises in harmonic minor, D♭ falls in Phrygian dominant.
  • Step 8: home, C.

The other steps can be any note in the scale. On the grid each note appears in several octaves; only its name counts.

02 States and moves

In reinforcement learning, the state is everything the agent knows when it makes a decision. Here it knows two things: which square it is standing on (64 possibilities) and which step of the sequence it is about to play (8). That makes 64 × 8 = 512 states, the 8 layers of the Q table.

The step is part of the state because the rewards depend on it: the same note can earn different points on different steps. This is how the agent learns not just to stay in the scale but to give each sequence its shape.

The actions are a chess king's moves: one square in any of the 8 directions. Moves off the board are not offered, and neither is the move straight back to the square it has just left, which stops it from bouncing between two notes forever.

So the full Q table holds 512 states × 8 moves = 4,096 values: for every state, how good each move looks. The cube shows one tile per state; in its Max Q view, each tile is lit by its best move.

03 Rewards

Points are the agent's only teacher:

  • +10: C on step 8.
  • +5: the third on step 1, G on step 4, and B or D♭ on step 7 (harmonic minor, Phrygian dominant).
  • 0: any other note in the scale.
  • −5: a target step that misses its note.
  • −10: any note outside the scale.

The −10 teaches the scale; the targets teach the shape. Other notes earn nothing on purpose: paying for them taught an earlier version to bounce between two cheap notes for ever.

By design, only a few key steps frame each sequence; the others leave room for variety and earn nothing on their own. So the agent cannot judge a move by its points alone. It judges a move by everything that follows from it: the points now plus all the points after, each counted a little less the further away it is. How much less is set by the discount factor γ.

The same idea, written one step at a time: the value of a move is its points now plus γ (0.92) times the value of the best move after it, max Q(s′, a′). For perfect play this is the Bellman equation (Richard Bellman, 1957), shown on the right. Q*(s, a) is the true value of move a in state s. The agent never knows it; section 04 shows how it closes in on it.

04 Learning: one-step Q-learning

The agent keeps updating its Q table. After each move it updates exactly one entry, the move it has just made, using the rule on the right. The rule comes from the Bellman equation in section 03: its right-hand side, computed with the agent's own estimates in place of Q*, becomes the target, called the TD target.

The bracket is the TD error: the gap between that target and the old estimate, or how wrong the old estimate turned out to be. α = 0.2 moves the estimate a fifth of the way towards the target; γ = 0.92 is the discount factor. As the errors shrink on the moves it keeps making, its estimates there settle on Q*.

To choose a move, the agent is ε-greedy: with probability ε it moves at random to explore; otherwise it takes the move with the highest Q value. ε starts at 1 and falls towards 0.02 as it practises. Drag the ε slider to take over.

An episode is one 8-step sequence. +2,000 episodes plays 16,000 moves silently in a fraction of a second; two presses are usually enough for any of the three scales.

05 What you hear

Each move plays the note under the agent as an eighth note at the chosen tempo; the bigger the surprise (the TD error), the louder the note. Early on almost every note is a surprise, and the music is loud and scattered. Once the agent has learned, its predictions come true, the notes soften, and the same sequences come back again and again.

Many routes score equally well, so each run settles on its own tune. Press Reset and train again to hear another.
